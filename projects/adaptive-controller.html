Adaptive wearable gaming controller

A wrist-worn controller for players with limited hand mobility. It turns wrist gestures into standard gamepad inputs, so it works with games people already own. Built with a team of four in 36 hours.

The problem

Standard controllers assume two hands with full finger control. For players with limited mobility, that rules out most games before they even start.

Our goal was a controller built around the movement a player does have. Wrist motion is often still available when fine finger control isn't.

How we approached it

We made one early decision that shaped everything else: the controller would identify itself to the computer as a standard HID gamepad. No drivers, no custom game support. If a game takes a controller, it takes ours.

That meant everything, from sensing to deciding what a gesture means, had to happen on the ESP32 itself.

Build log

  1. Read the IMU

    We started with raw accelerometer and gyroscope data from an IMU on the wrist. Neither works on its own. The accelerometer knows which way is down but is noisy whenever your arm moves. The gyroscope is smooth but drifts over time.

  2. Fuse the sensors into an orientation

    I wrote sensor fusion that combines both: the gyroscope for fast, smooth motion, and the accelerometer to correct its slow drift. The result is a stable estimate of wrist orientation, updated 300 times per second.

  3. Turn orientation into gestures

    With a clean orientation signal, gesture recognition became thresholds and timing windows. A tilt held past a threshold for long enough is a gesture, and a brief wobble isn't.

    Because everyone's range of motion is different, each user calibrates their own neutral position and limits. We exposed six gesture slots that can be mapped to any button.

  4. Fix the lag

    Our first full build worked, but felt sluggish in game. The problem was the order of operations: gestures were detected in one pass and reports sent in a later one.

    I restructured the firmware so detection and the HID report happen in the same loop iteration. Latency dropped to 12 ms, below what a player can feel.

  5. Test and demo

    We tested each of the six gestures repeatedly and reached 98% recognition accuracy, then demoed the controller live in a real game at judging.

What I learned

Accessibility design starts with the user's range of motion, not the device's. Per-user calibration did more for accuracy than any filtering change.

In a hackathon, the decision to use standard HID saved us. It removed a whole category of work and let us spend our time on the part that mattered.
