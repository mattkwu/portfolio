Autonomous monitor tracking system

A monitor that turns to follow me around the room, whether I'm at my desk, on a call, or working out in front of it. The camera and vision run on my laptop. The motion runs on an ESP32.

The problem

My monitor sits on a fixed arm, but I don't sit still. I study at the desk, take calls pacing around, and follow workout videos a few meters back. Every time I moved, the screen was pointed at the wrong place.

I wanted the monitor to keep facing me on its own, smoothly enough that I wouldn't notice it moving, and fast enough that it never lagged behind.

How I approached it

I split the system in two. Seeing is heavy work, so detection runs in Python on a laptop. Moving needs tight, predictable timing, so control runs in C++ on an ESP32. A serial link carries one number between them: where the monitor should point.

That split meant I could iterate on vision without touching firmware, and tune the motor without re-running the camera.

Build log

  1. Get detection working on a laptop

    I started with OpenCV person detection on a plain webcam feed and drew boxes over myself. It worked, but the box center jumped a few pixels every frame, and it disappeared entirely when I turned sideways.

    Pointing a motor straight at that raw position would have made the monitor shake.

  2. Smooth it with a Kalman filter

    I added a Kalman filter with a constant-velocity model. Each frame it blends where it predicted I'd be with where the detector says I am, weighted by how much it trusts each one.

    Two things improved at once. The jitter mostly disappeared, and when detection dropped out for a few frames the filter kept predicting my position instead of losing me. That's what brought target lock up to 95%.

    Tuning the noise values was the real work. Trust the detector too much and the jitter comes back. Trust the model too much and the monitor lags behind fast movements.

  3. Close the loop on the ESP32

    The laptop sends a target angle over serial. The ESP32 runs a PID loop at 200 Hz to drive the pan motor to it.

    My first gains overshot and oscillated around the target. I tuned in order: proportional until it responded quickly, derivative to damp the overshoot, then a small integral term to remove the last bit of steady-state offset. The goal was a move that feels deliberate, not twitchy.

  4. Chase down latency

    Even with good control, the monitor felt late. I timed each stage separately: frame capture, detection, filtering, serial, and motor response.

    Most of the delay was in vision, so I lowered the capture resolution to what detection actually needed and kept serial messages small. End to end, camera to motion, it came in under 75 ms.

  5. Test it in real use

    I ran 20+ sessions doing what I actually do in front of the monitor: studying, video calls, and workouts. I logged where it lost me and fixed those cases one at a time.

    It now tracks reliably out to about 3 meters, which covers the whole space I use.

What I learned

Most of the difficulty wasn't in any one component. It was in the seams: noisy detections feeding a controller, and latency stacking up across stages. Measuring each stage on its own was what made the problem solvable.

Next, I'd move detection onto the device itself, so the system doesn't need a laptop at all.
